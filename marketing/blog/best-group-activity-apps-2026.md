## DateNight / TogetherPlans

**Best for:** Deciding *what to do* together — couples, friend groups, family outings.

DateNight (the app at togetherplans.app) was built for a specific, frustrating scenario: two or more people want to do something together but can't agree on what. The app's core mechanic is a **private proposal + voting** model that sidesteps the social dynamics that make group decisions hard.

Here's the flow:
1. Each person proposes one or more activity ideas privately
2. All proposals go into a shared pool
3. Everyone votes on the options
4. The highest-voted activity wins

What makes this genuinely useful is what's absent: there's no group chat thread where one loud voice dominates, no pressure to justify your preference, and no one person stuck being the decider. The mechanism is inherently democratic without requiring anyone to campaign for their idea.

**Where it excels:**
- Couples deciding on a date night (the primary use case, and it shows in the UX)
- Small groups (4–8 people) deciding between activity options
- Situations where you want everyone's genuine preference rather than social agreement
- Cases where someone in the group tends to defer constantly — the private proposal gives them space to actually say what they want

**Where it doesn't fit:**
- Scheduling coordination (it doesn't handle availability/calendar matching — that's Doodle's lane)
- Very large groups (20+ people) where the noise of too many proposals becomes its own problem
- Text-heavy discussion (it's a decision tool, not a chat)
- If you need a persistent group chat alongside planning, you'll still want GroupMe or iMessage

**Verdict:** For the "what are we doing tonight?" problem, this is the most elegant solution in the category. It's free, the voting mechanic actually works, and it removes the social friction that makes group decisions drag. If your problem is *choosing an activity*, start here.

## Doodle

**Best for:** Figuring out when everyone is available before you even decide what to do.

Doodle has been solving the scheduling coordination problem since 2007, and it's genuinely good at it. You propose a set of dates and times, send a link, and people click which slots work for them. Doodle then shows you the overlap.

The free tier is functional but shows ads and lacks some features (automatic meeting booking, calendar integration) that live in the paid tier ($6.95/month in 2026).

**Where it excels:**
- Scheduling meetings across large groups
- Finding availability for recurring events
- Corporate or professional contexts where calendar coordination is the bottleneck

**Where it doesn't fit:**
- Deciding what to do (it's purely for when)
- Activity-specific planning
- Casual social contexts where a Doodle poll feels overly formal

**Verdict:** If you need to know *when* people are free before you can plan *what* to do, Doodle is the right first step. It's complementary to DateNight, not a competitor — use Doodle to lock down the window, then use DateNight to decide what happens in it.

## GroupMe

**Best for:** Keeping a persistent group conversation active over time.

GroupMe is a group messaging app with a decade of history, cross-platform compatibility, and one key advantage over iMessage or WhatsApp: it works equally well on iOS and Android without requiring everyone to have the same phone.

Functionally, it's a group chat. There's a poll feature built in, which sounds relevant — but it's a basic thumbs-up/thumbs-down poll, not a ranked or weighted voting system.

**Where it excels:**
- Long-lived friend groups that need a consistent shared space
- Large groups where iMessage can't include everyone (Android vs iPhone)
- Social coordination over time rather than for a single decision
- Communities, teams, clubs

**Where it doesn't fit:**
- Making a single decision cleanly (the chat format produces noise, not consensus)
- Groups where one or two people dominate — there's no structure to balance voices
- Activity planning beyond "hey, does Sunday work for everyone?"

**Verdict:** GroupMe is the right tool if you need a persistent home base for a group. It's not a decision-making tool. Most friend groups that use GroupMe also end up using something else to actually make decisions within it — often just texting the subgroup.

## Google Forms

**Best for:** Structured, anonymous surveys when you want quantitative answers.

Google Forms is not designed for social planning, but it's surprisingly capable if you build it right. You can create a ranked-choice ballot, collect everyone's top three preferences, and view the results in Sheets. It's flexible and free.

The catch is setup time. Creating a good decision-making Form requires thought — you have to anticipate the options, structure the questions, share the link, chase people to respond, and then interpret the results yourself. It's a survey tool cosplaying as a decision tool.

**Where it excels:**
- Large groups where an anonymous, formal vote is appropriate
- Recurring decisions (what theme should our annual party have?)
- Situations where you want the data in a spreadsheet for analysis
- Offices, clubs, or organizations that want a paper trail

**Where it doesn't fit:**
- Casual, spontaneous group decisions
- Any context where setup time is a barrier (which is most Friday evenings)
- Situations requiring back-and-forth or adding options mid-process

**Verdict:** Google Forms is powerful but has real friction. It's a great option when the decision is important enough to warrant structured input, but overkill for "where are we getting dinner tonight."

## Just Texting

**Best for:** Small groups, fast decisions, people who already know each other's preferences.

There's something to be said for the classics. A text thread with three close friends can resolve a plan in five minutes if everyone is responsive and decisive. No app required. No signup friction. Perfectly fine.

The problem with texting scales with the group. At 4 people it's manageable. At 6–8 it starts to fragment — side conversations happen, some people don't see messages, someone's suggestion gets buried under reactions, and the loudest voice often wins by default rather than by preference.

**Where it excels:**
- Pairs or trios of people who know each other extremely well
- Last-minute decisions where speed matters more than fairness
- Situations where one person is clearly the planner and just needs buy-in

**Where it doesn't fit:**
- Groups larger than 5 (signal-to-noise ratio collapses)
- Decisions where quiet group members have genuine preferences — texting favors the assertive
- Any situation involving date-time coordination (this becomes an iCal thread of chaos)

**Verdict:** Texting is zero-friction but produces zero structure. It works when the group dynamics are already resolved. When they're not — when you genuinely don't know what everyone wants — texting performs poorly.

## How to Choose

Here's a simple decision tree:

- **Scheduling when to meet?** → Doodle
- **Deciding what to do (couple or small group)?** → DateNight / TogetherPlans
- **Need a persistent group chat over time?** → GroupMe
- **Running a formal structured vote with many options?** → Google Forms
- **3 people, you know each other well, it's Friday night?** → Just text

In practice, the best group planners use two tools: **Doodle** to find the window, then **DateNight** to fill it. Everything else — GroupMe, Forms, texting — has its place, but they're solving adjacent problems.
